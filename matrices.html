Case 01 · Core math 01

One matrix bends the whole plane

A 2×2 matrix is a recipe for moving every point in space. Its two columns are where the x and y axes land, and the grid between them bends into parallelograms. Rotation, scale and shear are all the same operation with different numbers.

x' = ax + by · y' = cx + dy ↖ Move cursor
Case 02 · Sprites 02

Four numbers rotate and scale a sprite

A renderer never rotates pixels. It multiplies each vertex by a rotation matrix — cosine and sine arranged in two columns — then applies scale. The unit square becomes a parallelogram whose edges are the transformed basis vectors.

x' = x cosθ − y sinθ · y' = x sinθ + y cosθ Adjust the sliders
Case 03 · Translation 03

Translation hides in the third column

Translation doesn't fit in a 2×2 matrix, so engines add a third column and write points as (x, y, 1). Now one 3×3 multiply can rotate and move a character at once. That extra 1, called a homogeneous coordinate, is the whole trick.

p' = T(t) · R(θ) · p ↖ Move cursor
Case 04 · Composition 04

Order changes the answer

Matrices multiply in order, and order matters. Rotate-then-translate leaves a ship somewhere different from translate-then-rotate. The gap grows with the sine of the angle — the bug behind a muzzle flash that refuses to sit on the barrel.

T · R · p ≠ R · T · p
Case 05 · Cameras 05

The camera is an inverse matrix

A camera doesn't move the world; it moves the world into view space with the inverse of its own transform. For a rigid camera, that inverse is just a transpose plus a dot product — no division, no trigonometry beyond the heading.

M_view = R(−θ) · T(−p) ↖ Move cursor
Case 06 · Shear 06

Shear is free squash-and-stretch

Shear slides each row sideways in proportion to its height. Combined with non-uniform scale it gives you leaning cards, italic UI, jelly enemies and fake 3D — usually with no extra sprites. The determinant tracks how much area survives.

[1 k; 0 1] · [x; y] = [x + ky; y] Adjust the sliders
Case 07 · Projection 07

3D is a 4×4 and a divide

A 3D vertex goes through model, view and projection matrices, then gets divided by w. That one divide is why distant buildings shrink. Four numbers per row, one perspective divide, and a flat screen starts to look deep.

Drag the city to orbit
clip = P · V · M · p · ndc = clip / w Drag to orbit
Case 08 · Hierarchy 08

Bones are matrices inside matrices

Each bone stores only its local transform. Multiply it by every ancestor and you get world space. Rotate the torso and the shoulder, elbow and hand follow automatically — the same stack that drives characters, weapons, robot arms and camera rigs.

M_world = M_body · M_shoulder · M_elbow ↖ Move cursor

Pocket reference

The matrices behind the demos

Eight expressions that cover most gameplay transforms. Engines may store them column-major; the arithmetic is the same.

Rotate [ cosθ −sinθ ]
[ sinθ cosθ ]

Two columns, two basis vectors. Positive angles usually turn counter-clockwise in math, clockwise on screen.

Scale + shear [ sx k ] · [ x ]
[ 0 sy] [ y ]

Scale changes area. Shear leans without changing it — which is why the determinant ignores k.

Translate [ 1 0 tx ]
[ 0 1 ty ]
[ 0 0 1 ]

The third column is where translation lives. Points become (x, y, 1) so the multiply can add.

Compose M = T · R · S
p' = T(R(S · p))

Read right to left: scale first, then rotate, then translate. Swap any two and the result changes.

Hierarchy M_world = M_parent · M_local

Walk up the skeleton multiplying as you go. Every child inherits its parent's motion for free.

Inverse rigid V⁻¹ = [ Rᵀ t ]
[ 0 1 ]

For an un-scaled camera, invert rotation by transposing it, then add the camera position back.

Project clip = P · V · M · p
ndc = clip.xy / clip.w

The perspective divide is the whole illusion. Near geometry has small w; far geometry has large w.

Determinant det = ad − bc

Area scale factor. Zero means the matrix flattened space; negative means it also flipped it.
